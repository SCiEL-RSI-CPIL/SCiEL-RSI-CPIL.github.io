<Sec id="idea" />

A system improves *recursively* when its improvements <span class="key">make it better at improving</span>. SCiEL keeps the language model fixed<sup class="fnref" id="fnref-1"><a href="#fn-1">1</a></sup> and improves everything around it. Around the model it builds a repertoire: findings, tools and learned rules for choosing the next edit. It splits its programs into *demes*, groups that evolve on their own and in parallel. When one deme learns something useful, SCiEL tests it and passes it to the other demes as a *finding*; at the end of a run, it passes the repertoire to the next run (<a class="cite" href="#ref-15">Henrich, 2016</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-20">Boyd and Richerson, 1985</a>).

<Sec id="steering" />

**Every attempt is a handful of choices: which program to start from, what kind of edit to ask for, which finding to show, which strategy to follow.** SCiEL does not leave these choices to chance or to a fixed rule. It draws each choice at random with set odds, and it learns the odds from its own record of what worked. Three layers set the odds. A layer takes over only when it does better than the layer below on attempts it has not seen:

- **Bandits**, from the first attempt. When a kind of edit has just improved a program, SCiEL chooses it more often; it still tries the other kinds now and then, and it forgets old successes.
- **A world model**, from about 60 attempts. Before SCiEL calls the language model, the world model predicts whether an attempt will improve its line, and SCiEL favours the attempts it rates highly.
- **A step policy**, from about 200 attempts. It can shift the odds of every choice by a small, bounded amount, but only when the record shows a clear gain.

<Tiers />

This is how SCiEL points its effort in the right direction. It does not ask the model the same question in the same way for the whole run. It asks more often about the parents, edits and findings that have just worked at this stage of the climb, and less often about what keeps failing. SCiEL still makes one choice in ten at random and records the odds of every choice. A lucky streak therefore cannot lock in the run, and the record stays a fair experiment; it is the same experiment we use to test culture in <a href="#culture">Section 5</a>. Most attempts still do not help, so the choice of which attempts to make matters.

<Sec id="why" />

**A single model, a single climber and a crowd that shares nothing all get stuck; SCiEL keeps climbing.** To show why SCiEL needs each of its parts, we use a toy landscape that is small enough to search thousands of times. Solving a hard problem is like a long climb where you see only one step ahead. If you ask one model many times, no answer builds on an earlier success. One climber builds on its successes but stops at the first bump. Several climbers who share nothing split the budget, and each stops at its own bump.

<Sec id="culture" />

**Receiving a finding from another deme raises the chance that an edit improves from 54% to 62%.** The toy landscape suggests that culture matters, and real runs let us test it directly. When a deme writes its next prompt, SCiEL flips a weighted coin to decide whether to include a finding from another deme, and it records the odds. Every run is therefore a randomised experiment. Across 31 runs and 1,022 such decisions:

<CultureTest />

The interval is wide: at 95% confidence, the gain lies between 1.7 and 13.7 points. This result shows the first step of recursion: what the system keeps makes it more likely to improve. Whether the gain grows as the system keeps more is the first question in <a href="#open">Section 9</a>.

<Sec id="top" />

**Across all edits, 30% to 80% beat their parent; starting from a program close to the run's best, the share drops in five of the six runs, to as low as 7%.** Shared lessons and unlikely ideas matter most here, and SCiEL is built for this stage. In the run of the film, 82 of 99 attempts did not beat the best so far (<a class="tc" href="#film" data-t="140">2:20</a>).

<Sec id="limits" />

Our experiments have clear limits, and here is what we plan to do about them.

- **Culture into weights.** Today SCiEL keeps what it learns outside the model, in prompts, tools and policies. Next, we want to put it into the model itself. Every run already records what this needs: each attempt and its outcome, the findings that passed their tests, and the methods that worked. We collect these records to fine-tune small models cheaply. To do this, we pair SCiEL with DeltaServe (<a class="cite" href="#ref-22">Chen et al., 2026</a>), which trains and serves LoRA adapters in the same inference engine while a run is going. The records we have collected so far are on the [results page](https://sciel-cpil.github.io/sciel-results/) and in the [arena](https://research-arena.small-pine-3af7.workers.dev/experiments).
- **More backends.** Most runs use a relatively small open model, Qwen3.8-27B served locally, and only occasionally a frontier model through a subscription. We plan to widen the set of backends and compare them under one protocol.
- **Beyond exact checkers.** Today SCiEL works on problems where a program can be scored exactly and cheaply, such as packings, bounds and codes. Next, we want it to work on real data, where a score is noisy or expensive to compute; we are building the first such tasks now.

**Evolving programs with language models** (<a class="cite" href="#ref-21">Romera-Paredes et al., 2024</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-3">Novikov et al., 2025</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-1">Sharma, 2025</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-2">Lange et al., 2025</a>) keep a population of programs and select them by score. SCiEL keeps the population too, and it also keeps what the population learned about how to improve; with the same model and budget it reaches higher scores and needs about half as many evaluations (<a href="#results">Section 3</a>).

**Learning with data that responds to the learner.** Strategic classification (<a class="cite" href="#ref-16">Hardt et al., 2016</a>) and performative prediction (<a class="cite" href="#ref-17">Perdomo et al., 2020</a>) study a rule deployed on a population that adapts to it; evolutionary prediction games (<a class="cite" href="#ref-18">Saig and Rosenfeld, 2025</a>) show that retraining on the current mix of groups drives all but one out, and leave dependencies between groups as an open question. In SCiEL, groups of programs interact and improve, and because SCiEL records the probability of every choice, we can measure the quantities these models only assume, as in the test of <a href="#culture">Section 5</a>.

**Learning from experience and cumulative culture.** Agents should learn from the experience they gather themselves, with rewards that come from the world (<a class="cite" href="#ref-19">Silver and Sutton, 2025</a>); here the reward is an exact checker. Cumulative culture depends on people copying others and then testing what they copied (<a class="cite" href="#ref-20">Boyd and Richerson, 1985</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-15">Henrich, 2016</a>); SCiEL makes this test explicit and controls its error rate.

<Sec id="open" />

1. **Is the improvement recursive or cumulative?** A repertoire that adds a constant boost makes progress cumulative; only one that grows with each record bends the curve. A random share of attempts run without the repertoire answers it, and we are adding that arm.
2. **When do another group's findings beat a group's own?** In models of one rule shared by many groups, that decides whether transmission keeps the groups together or speeds the takeover by one. The logged offers of findings measure it in real runs.
3. **How well does the system know itself?** The log-loss of its own forecasts bounds how far it has fitted the checker rather than the problem.
